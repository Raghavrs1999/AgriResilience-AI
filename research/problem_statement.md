Deep Research Prompt: AI Builder Cup — Global Problem Discovery, Regional Gap Analysis, and Winning AI Solution Strategy
1. Research Objective
I want to conduct comprehensive, evidence-based research to identify the most promising problem areas and potential AI-powered solutions for the AI Builder Cup hackathon.
Official theme page: https://aibuildercup.com/themes.html
The primary objective is to understand the domains covered by the hackathon, investigate the most significant real-world problems and unmet needs within each domain, and identify opportunities where an innovative AI solution could create meaningful, measurable, and scalable impact.
The research must cover three geographical levels:
Global: Worldwide challenges, emerging opportunities, technological gaps, and unmet needs.
Asia: Region-specific challenges, market conditions, infrastructure limitations, and opportunities for AI-driven innovation.
South Asia and Japan and Asia-Pacific (JAPAC ) region: Localized challenges, underserved populations, regional constraints, and opportunities for solutions that can originate in or be deployed across these markets.
Investigate every domain listed on the official AI Builder Cup themes page without prematurely narrowing the research to a particular domain or technology.
The final outcome should help us make an informed decision about what to build, which problem to solve, why the problem matters, who would benefit, and how our solution could stand out in the competition while delivering real-world impact.
2. Research Area A: Understand Every AI Builder Cup Domain
First, identify and document every domain, theme, and subtheme listed on the official hackathon page.
For each domain, investigate:
What the domain encompasses and why it matters.
Its current global landscape, including major developments and emerging trends.
The most important problems faced by individuals, businesses, governments, and institutions operating in that domain.
Existing solutions, technologies, products, and organizations addressing these problems.
Limitations of existing approaches and problems that remain unsolved or inadequately addressed.
The role AI currently plays in the domain and where its adoption remains limited.
Opportunities for AI-driven innovation, including opportunities involving LLMs, multimodal AI, computer vision, predictive analytics, AI agents, automation, and other relevant techniques.
Technical, economic, regulatory, ethical, infrastructure, and adoption barriers.
The potential for a solution developed during a hackathon to become a useful, scalable real-world product.
Do not assume every domain has equal potential. Assess each independently using evidence, practical constraints, and the potential magnitude of the problems it addresses.
3. Research Area B: Global, Asian, and South Asian/JAPAC  Gap Analysis
For every domain, identify and compare the challenges across the following geographical levels.
Global perspective
What are the largest and most persistent problems worldwide?
Which problems remain unsolved despite significant investment and technological progress?
Where are current solutions too expensive, inaccessible, inaccurate, inefficient, fragmented, or difficult to scale?
What emerging challenges are likely to become more important over the next three to five years?
Asia-wide perspective
Which challenges are particularly significant in Asian markets?
How do population density, urbanization, industrial development, digital infrastructure, language diversity, and economic disparities influence these problems?
Which global solutions fail to translate effectively to Asian contexts, and why?
Where is there a gap between technological availability and actual adoption?
South Asia and Asia-Pacific perspective
Identify region-specific problems affecting countries such as japan, India, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Indonesia, the Philippines, Vietnam, and other relevant JAPAC  markets.
Investigate challenges involving affordability, accessibility, language diversity, fragmented data, infrastructure limitations, informal economies, local regulations, and underserved communities.
Identify problems where existing global products are unsuitable, prohibitively expensive, or insufficiently localized.
Examine opportunities to develop solutions in India or South Asia that could subsequently scale across other Asian markets and globally.
Treat South Asia and JAPAC  as distinct but potentially overlapping analytical areas. Clearly define the countries included in each geographical grouping and explain any relevant terminology used by the hackathon organizer.
For every identified gap, explain:
What the problem is.
Who experiences it.
Why existing solutions do not adequately solve it.
How widespread or severe the problem is.
Why the problem persists.
Whether AI can realistically address it.
Whether the opportunity is specific to one region or scalable internationally.
4. Research Area C: Historical Analysis of AI Builder Cup Hackathons
Investigate the available historical records of AI Builder Cup and its previous editions.
Search for official announcements, archived theme pages, competition results, winner announcements, project repositories, finalist presentations, participant demonstrations, interviews, and other credible sources.
For every identifiable past edition, document:
Edition name, year, and competition format.
Official themes and problem statements.
Winning teams and their project names.
The problems their projects attempted to solve.
Their proposed solutions and how those solutions worked.
The AI technologies, models, tools, platforms, and infrastructure they used, where verifiable.
The target users and intended beneficiaries.
The demonstrated functionality, evidence of impact, and maturity of the project.
The judging criteria and reasons for winning, where publicly documented.
The availability of a working prototype, public repository, demo, or deployed product.
Whether the project progressed beyond the hackathon into adoption, commercialization, further development, or measurable real-world impact.
Also investigate finalists, runners-up, and notable non-winning projects where information is available.
Historical analysis requirements
Do not merely list winners. Analyze the underlying patterns.
Determine:
Which problem categories repeatedly attract winning projects.
Which types of solutions appear to perform well.
Whether winning projects tend to prioritize novelty, technical execution, usability, measurable impact, scalability, or alignment with sponsor objectives.
Which technologies or solution architectures recur across successful projects.
What differentiates winners from other submissions, based on available evidence.
Whether previous editions reveal underserved themes or recurring problems that remain inadequately addressed.
Which earlier ideas could be improved through new technology, better implementation, regional adaptation, or a substantially different approach.
Distinguish between documented judging feedback and your own analytical interpretation.
If historical information is unavailable, explicitly identify the missing records. Do not invent winners, proposals, evaluation criteria, or project outcomes. State which editions and sources were actually verified.
5. Research Area D: Competitive Landscape and Existing Solutions
For each promising problem identified, investigate the current competitive and technological landscape.
Identify relevant:
Startups and established companies.
Open-source projects and research initiatives.
Academic research and emerging techniques.
Government or nonprofit initiatives.
AI-powered products and platforms.
Existing solutions popular in developed markets but unavailable, inaccessible, or unsuitable in the target regions.
For each major existing solution, assess:
The problem it solves.
Its intended users and geographic coverage.
Its technical approach.
Its strengths and limitations.
Its cost and accessibility, where information is available.
Evidence of effectiveness or adoption.
Remaining unmet needs.
The goal is not simply to discover ideas that have never been attempted. It is to identify opportunities where a meaningfully better, more accessible, more affordable, more accurate, or more context-aware solution is possible.
Distinguish genuine market gaps from problems that are already adequately addressed by existing products.
6. Research Area E: Identify High-Potential AI Opportunities
Using the findings from all previous research areas, generate a shortlist of promising problem statements that could be addressed through an AI Builder Cup submission.
For every proposed opportunity, provide:
Problem statement: A specific, clearly defined real-world problem.
Target users: The people, organizations, or communities affected.
Evidence of need: Statistics, research, case studies, or credible reports establishing the problem's significance.
Current alternatives: Existing products, manual processes, or workarounds.
Unmet need: What existing alternatives fail to address.
Proposed AI solution: A concrete concept for addressing the problem.
AI necessity: Why AI is useful or necessary, rather than adding AI without a clear purpose.
Technical feasibility: Potential models, datasets, tools, APIs, infrastructure, and integrations.
Prototype scope: What can realistically be built and demonstrated within a hackathon.
Differentiation: Why the proposed approach would be meaningfully better than existing alternatives.
Measurable impact: The metrics that could demonstrate improvement.
Scalability: How the solution could expand across users, industries, countries, or languages.
Risks and limitations: Technical, ethical, regulatory, data availability, privacy, and deployment concerns.
Long-term potential: Whether the project could evolve into a sustainable product or platform.
Explore both globally relevant problems and opportunities originating in South Asia or JAPAC  that could eventually achieve global adoption.
Do not limit the analysis to conventional chatbots or generic AI assistants. Consider complete workflows, AI-enabled infrastructure, decision-support systems, intelligent automation, multimodal applications, domain-specific agents, and solutions that combine AI with existing systems.
7. Research Area F: Hackathon Feasibility and Winning Potential
A problem can be important without being suitable for a hackathon. Evaluate each shortlisted idea against both its real-world value and its ability to become a compelling working demonstration.
Assess:
Alignment with the official competition themes and rules.
Novelty and differentiation from previous winning projects.
Severity and scale of the problem.
Strength of evidence supporting the need.
Technical feasibility within the competition timeframe.
Availability of suitable data, APIs, models, and development tools.
Ability to demonstrate a working end-to-end workflow.
Quality of the user experience.
Measurable performance and impact.
Potential for a memorable, convincing live demonstration.
Scalability beyond the prototype.
Practical deployment costs and operational sustainability.
Risks of relying on third-party APIs, proprietary platforms, or unavailable datasets.
Review the official judging rubric and competition constraints wherever published. If the current edition has sponsor-specific technologies, required platforms, eligibility rules, or other restrictions, account for them explicitly.
Do not claim that a project will win. Instead, estimate its competitive strengths and weaknesses using a transparent evaluation framework.
8. Research Methodology and Evidence Standards
Conduct the research systematically rather than relying on general knowledge or a collection of superficial web summaries.
Prioritize sources in this order:
Official AI Builder Cup pages, rules, archives, announcements, and judging criteria.
Government datasets, international organizations, and official statistics.
Peer-reviewed research, academic publications, and reputable research institutions.
Credible industry reports and market studies.
Company documentation, product demonstrations, and public repositories.
Reputable journalism and documented practitioner experiences.
Use sources such as the World Bank, United Nations, WHO, OECD, regional development institutions, relevant government agencies, and established research organizations when appropriate to the domain.
For every important claim:
Provide a citation and a direct source link.
Record the publication date and the period covered by the data where possible.
Prefer recent evidence for current conditions while retaining older foundational research where relevant.
Distinguish facts, estimates, assumptions, and analytical judgments.
Cross-check important findings against independent sources.
Identify conflicting evidence and explain material uncertainties.
Avoid unsupported market-size estimates and unverified impact claims.
Do not fabricate historical competition information.
Explicitly identify research limitations, missing data, inaccessible archives, and areas where conclusions are tentative.

10. Expected Deliverables
Present the final research as a structured report with the following sections.
Deliverable 1: Executive Summary
Summarize the most important findings, emerging opportunities, major gaps, and recommended strategic direction.
Deliverable 2: Complete Domain Map
List every official hackathon domain and its subthemes, accompanied by a concise explanation of its scope and major challenges.
Deliverable 3: Global and Regional Gap Analysis
Compare the challenges in each domain across global, Asian, South Asian, and broader JAPAC  contexts.
Deliverable 4: Historical Hackathon Analysis
Provide an edition-by-edition record of previous winners, their proposals, available implementation details, and lessons learned.
Deliverable 5: Competitive Landscape
Document existing solutions and identify their remaining limitations.
Deliverable 6: Opportunity Matrix
Create a comparative table of the most promising problems, affected users, evidence of need, AI opportunities, regional relevance, and estimated impact.
Deliverable 7: Ranked Shortlist
Rank the top 10 potential problem statements using the scoring framework. Explain the strengths, weaknesses, assumptions, and uncertainties behind the ranking.
Deliverable 8: Top Three Solution Blueprints
For the three strongest opportunities, provide:
A detailed problem statement.
Target users and user journey.
Proposed solution architecture.
Relevant AI models, tools, APIs, and datasets.
Core features and a realistic MVP scope.
End-to-end demonstration workflow.
Evaluation metrics and baseline comparisons.
Differentiation from existing solutions and previous hackathon winners.
Major technical and deployment risks.
A credible path from hackathon prototype to real-world adoption.
Deliverable 9: Final Recommendation
Recommend the strongest opportunity to pursue, explain why it is preferable to the alternatives, and identify what additional validation is needed before committing development effort.
Include a backup option in case the preferred idea proves infeasible.
Deliverable 10: Action Plan
Provide a practical sequence for moving from research to implementation:
Validate the problem.
Interview or study target users.
Confirm data and technical feasibility.
Define the MVP.
Build the prototype.
Test against measurable baselines.
Prepare the demonstration and final submission.
11. Final Decision-Making Principle
The goal is not to produce the largest number of AI ideas or choose the trendiest technology.
The goal is to identify a problem that is genuinely important, demonstrably underserved, technically addressable, feasible to prototype, clearly differentiated from existing solutions, and capable of delivering meaningful impact beyond the hackathon.
Prioritize problem-first innovation, evidence over assumptions, working demonstrations over ambitious but incomplete concepts, and measurable outcomes over AI novelty alone.
Begin by inspecting the official AI Builder Cup theme page and verifying the current competition requirements. Then conduct the historical, domain-level, regional, and competitive research before generating and ranking solution ideas.
Do not recommend a final project until the evidence from these research stages has been synthesized.
